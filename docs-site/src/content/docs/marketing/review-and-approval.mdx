Handing work over ends here: you get a link, you read what was built, and you decide. The
review is the one step cckit never does for you. A piece is done when a person other than whoever
asked for it has read it and said yes.

A review has one place, one link and one of two endings, so nobody has to ask whether the piece
was ever approved.

## Who does it

- **The writer**, the person who handed the work over, applies the changes with your assistant.
- **The approver** reads, comments and decides. A GitHub account and a browser are enough.
- **Your assistant** prepares the review, applies comments when asked and reports the ones it did
  not apply. It never approves.

## Someone sent you a review link

You are the approver. The link opens one page on GitHub, and that page is the review.

1. **Open the link** and sign in to GitHub if it asks.
2. **Open the Files changed tab.** It shows what the piece says. New text is marked in green.
3. **Comment where something is wrong.** Move the pointer over a line, click the **+** that
   appears and write your comment.
4. **Decide.** Click **Review changes** and choose **Approve** or **Request changes**.
5. **If you asked for changes, look again** when the writer says they are applied. The same page
   updates by itself. This repeats until your answer is **Approve**.

The writer's side is shorter to tell: they hand the piece over, send you the link, and apply your
comments with their assistant. What they say is on
[Hand over your first piece](/marketing/your-first-piece/) and
[Ask for changes to a piece](/marketing/ask-for-changes/).

## What the two answers mean

| Answer | Means | What happens next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Approve** | The piece can become part of the shared version as it is. | The writer tells their assistant to finish the piece. |
| **Request changes** | The piece is not ready. The comments say why. | The writer applies them with their assistant; the approver reads again. |

A comment alone is not an answer. A piece with comments and no decision is still waiting.

## Why the writer never approves their own piece

GitHub enforces it: the person who asked for a review cannot approve it. The rule exists for the
reason at the top of this page. It also means every approved piece has a named second reader,
and the project's history shows who that was.

On a team of two, each person approves the other's pieces. One person can be the writer of one
piece and the approver of another.

### If you work alone

A team of one has no second reader, and GitHub will not let you click **Approve** on a piece you
asked for. You read the review yourself and finish it: tell your assistant the piece is fine, or
use the green **Merge pull request** button on the review page. What you give up is the second
pair of eyes, so read the Files changed tab as carefully as an approver would. To get a second
reader, give one other person a GitHub account with access to the project.

## What else gets reviewed

- **A campaign plan.** Before any piece is written, the plan opens as a page in your browser
  where you comment and answer. See [Several pieces, one goal](/marketing/campaigns/).
- **The brand voice.** A change to it is reviewed like a piece, because it applies to everyone.
  See [Set your brand voice](/marketing/brand-voice/).

An automatic check can also run when a draft is saved, such as a check for a password left in a
file. If one fails, your assistant tells you "A check failed" and what failed. It is not an approval and
does not replace the approver.

## After approval

Once the review says **Approve**, the writer tells their assistant that it is approved:

<Chat
  title="finishing an approved Easter menu page"
  say="My colleague approved the Easter menu page. Can you wrap it up?"
  means="This is the writer's step. The approver can also finish it from the review page."
>
  Done. The Easter menu page is part of the shared version, the piece is marked done and I removed
  the draft, which is no longer needed.
</Chat>

Three things happen:

1. The approved draft becomes part of the shared version of the project.
2. The piece is marked done on the task list.
3. The draft is tidied away.

The approver can also finish it from the review page with the green **Merge pull request**
button, which some projects label **Squash and merge**.

The approved text is now a file in the project, and the shared version is the one place to copy
it from.

**cckit does not publish.** The result is an approved site that works; putting it on a public
address is a separate step, done by the technical person or the hosting service. It does not put the piece on your site, send the email, schedule the
post or tell you how it performed. Your team does those in the tools it already uses, the way it
does today. No publishing, scheduling or analytics tool is connected.
