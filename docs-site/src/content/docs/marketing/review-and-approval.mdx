A writer cannot see their own mistakes. They know what the sentence was meant to say, so they
read that and miss what it says. A piece is done when a person other than its writer has read it
and said yes.

Review also fails in a familiar way: comments arrive in a chat, an email and a hallway, and
nobody is sure whether the piece was ever approved. In this method a review has one place, one
link and one of two endings.

## Who does it

- **The writer** asks for the review and applies the changes, with Claude.
- **The approver** reads, comments and decides. A GitHub account and a browser are enough.
- **Claude** prepares the review, applies comments when asked and reports the ones it did not
  apply. It never approves.

## How a review runs

1. **The writer asks for it.** "This is ready. Save it and ask for a review." Claude saves the
   draft, sends it to GitHub and returns a link.
2. **The writer sends the link to the approver.** That link is the review: one page that shows
   what the piece says.
3. **The approver reads and comments.** On that page they open the **Files changed** tab and
   comment on any line.
4. **The approver decides.** They click **Review changes** and choose **Approve** or
   **Request changes**.
5. **If changes are needed, the writer applies them** and the page updates by itself. The
   approver looks again. This repeats until the answer is **Approve**.

The same steps from the writer's side, with the exact words to say to Claude, are on
[A piece, idea to approved](/marketing/your-first-piece/).

## What the two answers mean

| Answer | Means | What happens next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Approve** | The piece can become part of the shared version as it is. | The writer tells Claude to finish the piece. |
| **Request changes** | The piece is not ready. The comments say why. | The writer applies them with Claude; the approver reads again. |

A comment alone is not an answer. A piece with comments and no decision is still waiting.

## Why the writer never approves their own piece

GitHub enforces it: the person who asked for a review cannot approve it. The rule exists for the
reason at the top of this page. It also means every approved piece has a named second reader,
and the project's history shows who that was.

On a team of two, each person approves the other's pieces. One person can be the writer of one
piece and the approver of another.

## What else gets reviewed

- **A campaign plan.** Before any piece is written, the plan opens as a page in your browser
  where you comment and answer. See [Plan and run a campaign](/marketing/campaigns/).
- **The brand voice.** A change to it is reviewed like a piece, because it applies to everyone.
  See [Set your brand voice](/marketing/brand-voice/).

An automatic check can also run when a draft is saved, such as a check for a password left in a
file. If one fails, Claude tells you "A check failed" and what failed. It is not an approval and
does not replace the approver.

## After approval

Once the review says **Approve**, the writer tells Claude: "The review is approved. Finish this
piece." Three things happen:

1. The approved draft becomes part of the shared version of the project.
2. The piece is marked done on the task list.
3. The draft is tidied away.

The approver can also finish it from the review page with the green **Merge pull request**
button, which some projects label **Squash and merge**.

The approved text is now a file in the project, and the shared version is the one place to copy
it from.

**cckit does not publish.** It does not put the piece on your site, send the email, schedule the
post or tell you how it performed. Your team does those in the tools it already uses, the way it
does today. No publishing, scheduling or analytics tool is connected.

For a campaign there is one more step you can take later. When the goal's date has passed, tell
Claude the number you measured and it records it in the campaign plan. A campaign with no number
is recorded as not measured.
