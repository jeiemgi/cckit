<TutorialMeta />

A campaign is several pieces delegated under one goal: a landing page, three emails, five social
posts. They have to tell one story and be ready together. Planned under one goal, they show what
is written, what is stuck and what is left. At Pilot Light Bakery the Easter menu page is approved
and online; "Easter week" is the campaign that sends neighbours to it.

**Who does it:** you state the goal and approve the plan. The planner assistant proposes the
pieces and keeps the list. Each piece is then handed over like
[your first one](/marketing/your-first-piece/), one at a time.

**What you will have at the end:** an approved campaign plan saved in the project, every piece on
the task list, and each piece taken to approved.

**Before you start:** your [brand voice](/marketing/brand-voice/) is set, because the plan takes
its audience and its message from it. You have taken
[one piece to approved](/marketing/your-first-piece/), because every piece of a campaign moves the
same way.

<Steps>

1. **State the goal.** One goal, one audience and one date are enough to start. Your assistant
   asks for what is missing. A goal needs a number and a date; a second goal or a second audience
   is a second campaign.

   <Chat
     title="stating the goal of the Easter week campaign"
     say="I want to plan a campaign for Easter. The goal is 100 Easter loaf pre-orders by March 20. It is for the neighbours who follow our Instagram and are on the shop's mailing list."
     means="You gave one goal with a number and a date, one audience and no second goal."
   >
     That is one goal, one audience and one date. Before I plan: where does an order happen, at the
     counter, by phone or on the site? The plan needs to say where each piece sends people.
   </Chat>

2. **Read the plan.** Your assistant proposes the campaign: the message, the pieces, the order and
   what each piece is for. The plan opens as a page in your browser where you can comment and
   answer. When no browser is available, it is shown in the chat.

   <Chat
     title="reading the Easter week plan"
     say="Orders happen on the Easter menu page. Show me the plan."
     means="The page is for reading and answering. Nothing is created yet."
   >
     The plan is open in your browser. The Easter menu page is already approved, so it is in the
     plan as done. Two pieces are new: a post about the loaf and a post about the ordering
     deadline. Both send people to the menu page. Comment on any part and send your answer.
   </Chat>

3. **Change it until it is right.** Remove pieces you will not make. Add the ones it missed.
   Nothing has been created yet.

   <Chat
     title="changing the Easter week plan"
     say="Add a third post about collection day."
     means="Change the plan until you would be happy to approve it."
   >
     Added. The plan now has the menu page and three posts: the loaf, the ordering deadline and
     collection day. Nothing is created yet.
   </Chat>

4. **Approve the plan.** Your assistant records the campaign and one piece of work for each new
   piece in the plan. Nothing is recorded before you say yes.

   <Chat
     title="approving the Easter week plan"
     say="The plan is good. Go ahead and set it up."
     means="The plan and the list of its pieces go into one review."
   >
     The campaign "Easter week" and its three posts are written down. I opened one review with the
     plan and the list; here is the link for your colleague. Once it is approved, the three posts
     are on the shared list as not started.
   </Chat>

5. **Hand over the pieces.** Each piece goes the way
   [your first one](/marketing/your-first-piece/) did.

   <Chat
     title="starting the first post of Easter week"
     say="What is next in the campaign? Build it."
     means="One piece at a time, each with its own draft."
   >
     Next is the post about the Easter loaf. I have started it and I will tell you when there is a
     link to review.
   </Chat>

6. **Wait for the link.** Each piece comes back with its own review.

   <Chat
     title="getting the review link for the first Easter week post"
     say="How is the post about the loaf coming along?"
     means="Each piece has its own draft and its own review."
   >
     The post about the Easter loaf is ready, and here is the review link. Two posts are left: the
     ordering deadline and collection day.
   </Chat>

7. **Approved.** Once a piece is approved, tell your assistant so, and it finishes the piece (see
   [After approval](/marketing/review-and-approval/#after-approval)). Then ask for the next one,
   until none is left.

</Steps>

## Good to know

- **Research before you plan.** Ask your assistant to look at what competitors are saying, or to
  check a claim, before the plan is written. It reports its sources.
- **A plan holds at most eight pieces.** More than that is two campaigns.

<Aside type="note">
Sending the emails and scheduling the posts are outside cckit. The campaign ends when its pieces
are approved.
</Aside>

## After the campaign

When the goal's date has passed, tell your assistant the number you measured. It records it in
the plan. cckit does not measure it for you, and a campaign with no number is recorded as not
measured.

<Chat
  title="recording the Easter week result"
  say="Easter week is over. We took 112 pre-orders. Put that in the plan."
  means="The number is yours. cckit does not measure it."
>
  Recorded: 112 pre-orders against the goal of 100.
</Chat>

## If your technical person moved the task list to GitHub

The marketing setup keeps the task list in the project, and the steps above describe that. The
technical person can switch it to GitHub. If you are not sure which one you have, ask them.

<Outcomes title="What changes with the task list on GitHub">

| | Task list in the project | Task list on GitHub |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **When you approve the plan** | The plan and the list of its pieces go into one review. Once that review is approved, every piece is on the shared list as not started. | The campaign appears on GitHub with each piece under it. |
| **How the pieces are reviewed** | One at a time. Each piece has its own draft and its own review. | Together. The pieces share one draft and get one review at the end. |
| **How many at once** | No limit. | Two campaigns in progress is the default limit. The technical person can change it. |

- **Asking for the review.** With the task list on GitHub, ask once, when every piece is written:
  "The campaign is ready. Ask for the review." That one review covers all the pieces, so the
  approver reads them side by side and can check that they tell one story.
- **Finishing.** Once the campaign review says approved, say "The campaign review is approved.
  Finish the campaign." Your assistant makes the approved pieces part of the shared version and
  marks the campaign and every piece under it done.
- **Finish a campaign only when every piece is written.** With the task list on GitHub, finishing
  the campaign marks every piece under it done, including one nobody wrote. With the task list in
  the project, each piece is finished on its own, so an unwritten piece stays on the list.

</Outcomes>
